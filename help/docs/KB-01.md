# KB-01 · How a robot uses the map, and what a jump is

## Localisation in one paragraph

The robot's laser scanner measures the distance to everything around it many times a second. The robot compares that outline with the wall dots in its map and picks the position where the two agree best. It reports how well they agree as **confidence** (0 to 1). Between scans it also counts wheel turns, so it expects to be close to where it was a moment ago.

## What a jump is

A jump is the robot's reported position moving by tens of centimetres or more in an instant, without the robot moving that far.

It happens where the map contradicts itself. Suppose one part of the map is drawn 1.4 m away from where it really is relative to the rest. A robot driving from the good part into the wrong part first holds its position from the walls behind it. When those walls leave its view, only the misplaced walls are left to match, and the best agreement is now 1.4 m to the side. The position snaps over. The robot then steers to get back on its path, and it may stop, scrape something or report a localisation fault.

Driving the other way, the same thing happens at the same seam, in the opposite direction.

## Why the jump is at the seam and the cause is elsewhere

The jump shows where the robot switches from one piece of the map to the other. The fault is the placement of the whole piece. This is why repairing the walls at the jump does not help: the walls there are fine, they are just in the wrong place together with everything behind them.

## A piece attached at an angle

Maps are often extended by scanning a new area and joining it to the old map. If the join is off by a small angle, the new piece is right where it touches the old map (the pivot) and more and more wrong with distance. Two degrees is 3.5 cm per metre: after 40 m the piece is 1.4 m off.

When that piece forms a loop that meets the old map again at its far end, the far end is where the two disagree by a metre or more. That is where robots jump.

## Low confidence is not a jump

Confidence drops whenever the lasers see things the map does not have: pallets, bins, carts, people, an open dock door. That is normal and the robot keeps its position through it using the walls it still recognises and its wheel count. A map problem is when the position itself steps.

## Laser and reflector localisation

Some areas use reflectors: the robot finds its position from reflective markers instead of walls. Reflector areas are drawn as areas in the map. The wall-based tests in this program do not judge reflector areas; the recording summary marks where the robot was in reflector mode.
